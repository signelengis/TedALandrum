Before the Navy

Georgia Tech, 1941–1942

He came to Georgia Tech out of Tech High School in Atlanta, took aeronautical engineering, joined Sigma Nu, and learned to fly. Two things about the school in those years explain how an engineering student from Austell ended up in a Navy cockpit within a year of Pearl Harbor.

The first was the Daniel Guggenheim School of Aeronautics. Founded in 1930 with a $300,000 grant — the seventh and last of the seven aeronautics schools the Guggenheim family endowed across the country — it opened with eighteen students in a purpose-built building in 1931. When he enrolled it was barely ten years old. The school also ran a federally sponsored flight training program, proposed in the 1938–39 academic year, of the kind that put college students in the air and fed them into military aviation. That is the most likely place he learned.

Ted A. Landrum, U.S. Navy.

The second was the Naval ROTC. In 1926 the Navy chose six schools in the country to host a naval branch of the ROTC, and Georgia Tech was the only one in the South — picked in part for the strength of its mathematics teaching, and in spite of sitting three hundred miles from the ocean. By his time the unit trained in a Naval Armory built in 1935 that contained a full simulated ship’s bridge with a working communications system, an operational ship’s boiler, a lifeboat with davits, a flagbag and signal lights, and a four-inch naval gun. Midshipmen went to sea in the summers aboard battleships. The unit has commissioned more than three thousand officers.

Having been in ROTC is what let him go straight into Navy officer training for naval aviation rather than into the fleet or the draft. He went on active duty in 1942, a year or two into a four-year degree. The V-12 program, which let men finish an engineering degree in uniform, did not reach Tech until 1 July 1943 — after he was already flying. He never got the chance to go back and finish after the war, and it bothered him.

Lou Cobb, who would be his bunkmate at Pensacola, was also at Georgia Tech when the war started, and dated his own decision to fly for the Navy to the night of 7 December 1941 in his dorm room there.
The Cadet Year

Pensacola and Melbourne, 1943

The cadet in the next bunk was Lewis M. Cobb, of Bradenton, Florida. Cobb’s son later wrote up that winter from his father’s letters, diary and pilot’s logbook, and Ted appears in it by name — a Georgia Tech man out of Austell, described as one of the better pilots in the cadre and the sort who would answer an instructor’s question first and take the ribbing for it. Their bunks were side by side and they became friends.

The cadre had arrived out of primary training on biplanes and had to check out in the Vultee SNV: 450 horsepower against the Stearman’s 250, a radio, a two-position propeller, and a shimmy in certain configurations that got it called the Vultee Vibrator. Almost everyone was humbled by it in the first week.

Then came formation flying — four aircraft holding tight position through synchronized turns, the hardest thing they had been asked to do. On one hop the section leader rolled hard instead of gently and his wing went into the propeller of the aircraft beside him. Both went down. Two cadets they had trained alongside were killed in front of them.

The instructor ordered the whole cadre back into the air at 1300 that same afternoon. Ted objected. The instructor’s answer was that they were training to be combat pilots, that friends would die in front of them, and that grief had to wait. Three men left the program. The rest finished formation flying on 22 February 1943.

Melbourne, that summer

They finished Pensacola with the wings of gold and orders to NAS Melbourne — Operational Training Unit No. 2 — and four days of liberty, the first time off they had been given. Ted and Cobb checked fishing gear out of base Special Services and took the USO bus to Indialantic Beach, where Ted caught bluefish one after another on a hammered silver spoon while Cobb caught nothing. That evening the two of them went to a triple blind date at the Melbourne Country Club, where Cobb met the girl he would later marry.

Melbourne was where they stopped being students. The SNJ gave way to the Grumman F4F-3 Wildcat — 1,200 horsepower against the trainer’s 550, four guns instead of two, and landing gear you cranked up by hand, thirty turns, without letting go. They flew three and four hours most days: gunnery, instruments, night hops, navigation, and armed anti-submarine patrols off a coastline where U-boats had sunk five ships within fifty miles of the base the year before.

In late July their division drew a ferry mission: fly commercial to NAS Quonset Point in Rhode Island, sign out four Wildcats, and bring them back to Florida with no senior officer along. Their instructor’s briefing amounted to: you are officers, you have the training, go and do the job. Ted flew as Cobb’s wingman, call sign Falcon-four. They took the formation down to two thousand feet over the Statue of Liberty and made a couple of lazy circles over New York before turning south. A fuel leak put them into Wildwood overnight; they came home by way of Norfolk, Charleston and Jacksonville.

Off duty, Ted took their money. Cobb calls him his best friend and the undisputed Acey Deucey champion of the squadron, and describes losing a month’s pay to him a dollar at a time on a blanket in the barracks. In August the cadre finished carrier qualification — eight arrested landings aboard USS Sable, a converted Great Lakes excursion steamer standing in for a fleet carrier on Lake Michigan.

Aircraft of the training years
WWII · Primary Trainer

Boeing-Stearman N2S-3 Kaydet

Classic open-cockpit biplane used to train nearly every WWII Navy pilot.

Boeing built the Model 75 from 1934 and turned out more than ten thousand of them; the Navy called it the N2S, the Army the PT-13 and PT-17. The N2S-3 carried a 220-horsepower Continental radial, two open cockpits in tandem, and no flaps. Its narrow landing gear and long nose were deliberately unforgiving on the ground, and cadets who could not keep it straight on rollout did not continue. Painted training yellow, it was known throughout the Navy as the Yellow Peril.

Ted Landrum

He was flying Bureau Number 3500 on 3 September 1943 when it went down five miles northeast of Robertsdale, Alabama, at an outlying field of NAAS Bronson Field. The Navy logged it as a crash with injury, damage rating A — the aircraft was a write-off. He survived, continued training, and earned his wings.

WWII · Basic Trainer

Vultee SNV Valiant

The step between the Stearman and the Texan, and the aircraft his cadre checked out in at Pensacola in February 1943.

Known to the Army as the BT-13, the Valiant had 450 horsepower where the Stearman had 250, plus a closed canopy, a radio and a two-position propeller — enough new things at once that a cadet could get into trouble much faster than he was used to. It was heavier than anything they had flown and carried more momentum through a maneuver. Everyone called it the Vultee Vibrator, for the shimmy it developed in certain configurations. More than eleven thousand were built.

Ted Landrum

Flown at Pensacola from February 1943, including the formation training his cadre completed on 22 February.

WWII · Advanced Trainer

North American SNJ Texan

The Navy’s advanced trainer, and the aircraft that stood between the Stearman and a fleet cockpit.

Where the Stearman taught a cadet to fly, the SNJ taught him to fly like a naval aviator. It had everything the Stearman lacked — retractable landing gear, flaps, a constant-speed propeller, a closed canopy, a 600-horsepower Pratt & Whitney radial and a machine gun to learn gunnery with — and it was demanding enough that a student who could handle it could handle a fighter. Carrier-qualified versions had tailhooks, and cadets made their first arrested landings in them. The tips of its propeller went supersonic at full power, which is why anyone who trained near one remembers the sound.

Ted Landrum

Flown at Pensacola. Dates not recorded.

WWII · Carrier Fighter

Grumman F4F Wildcat

The first real fighter he flew, and the aircraft that carried the Navy through the first two years of the Pacific war.

Barrel-bodied, stubby and slower than what it was sent up against, the Wildcat survived on toughness and on tactics — the Thach Weave was invented to give pairs of them a way to beat the Zero. It had 1,200 horsepower where the SNJ had 550, four fifty-caliber guns where the trainer had two, and a ceiling almost twice as high. Its landing gear retracted by hand: thirty turns of a crank you did not dare let go of, since the handle could break your hand on the way back. Grumman’s airframes were famously hard to kill; the company was known in the fleet as the Grumman Iron Works.

Ted Landrum

Flown at NAS Melbourne through the summer of 1943, including the ferry flight home from Quonset Point in July.

Korean War

VC-11, USS Philippine Sea

From June 1950 to March 1951 he was deployed to Korea aboard the Essex-class carrier USS Philippine Sea with Composite Squadron 11, a detachment squadron that put small teams of specialist aircraft aboard carriers rather than deploying as a whole.

He flew Douglas Skyraiders in two configurations. The AD-4N was the night-attack aircraft, with radar, a searchlight and two crewmen behind the pilot. The AD-4W carried an enormous APS-20 radar in a belly radome and no weapons at all — it was the fleet’s eyes, extending the radar horizon far past what the ship itself could see.

Sent home from the deployment, he went to NAS Atlanta as Commanding Officer of VC-11 — his own squadron, in his own city, by then a Lieutenant Commander. He held the command about three years.

The AD-4W photograph in this site’s masthead is a VC-11 Det. I aircraft off Korea in about 1952. It is the one image here that shows his actual squadron.

Aircraft over Korea
Korean War · Airborne Early Warning

Douglas A-1 Skyraider (AD-4W)

Radar early-warning variant of the Skyraider, easily identified by the large belly-mounted radome.

The AD-4W traded its bomb load for an APS-20 search radar slung under the belly in a radome so large it changed the aircraft’s silhouette entirely. Two operators sat inside the fuselage watching the scope, extending a carrier group’s radar horizon far beyond what the ship itself could see. It was the Navy’s first practical carrier-borne early-warning aircraft, and the Royal Navy flew it too, as the Skyraider AEW.1.

Ted Landrum

This photo is specifically of a VC-11 AD-4W in flight off Korea circa 1952 — Landrum’s own squadron.

Korean War · Attack / Night-Attack

Douglas A-1 Skyraider (AD-4N)

Rugged single-engine attack aircraft; the AD-4N was the night-attack variant.

Ed Heinemann drew the Skyraider in the closing months of the Second World War as a single-engine bomber that could carry as much as a B-17 had over short distances. Fifteen hardpoints and a Wright R-3350 gave it up to eight thousand pounds of ordnance and hours of loiter over a target — the quality that kept it in service through Vietnam, long after jets had replaced everything around it. The AD-4N added search radar, a searchlight pod and two crewmen in the fuselage behind the pilot for work after dark.

Ted Landrum

Flown with VC-11 aboard USS Philippine Sea during the Korean War.

Carrier Fighter-Bomber

Vought F4U Corsair

Bent-wing Navy and Marine Corps fighter-bomber, in service from the Second World War through Korea. The F4U-4 shown here was the version flying from carriers off Korea.

The Corsair’s inverted gull wing existed to solve a problem: the Pratt & Whitney R-2800 up front swung a propeller more than thirteen feet across, and the wing had to be cranked down to give it ground clearance without absurdly long landing gear. Early examples bounced badly on carrier decks and the long nose blocked the pilot’s view of the LSO, so the Navy sent the first ones to Marine squadrons ashore before clearing the type for shipboard use. The F4U-4 of the Korean years put out 2,380 horsepower and worked mainly as a fighter-bomber. Production ran until 1953, longer than any other American piston fighter.

Ted Landrum

Squadron and dates not yet recorded.

1950s

Flight Test, NAS Norfolk

From Atlanta the Navy sent him to NAS Norfolk to run a squadron whose job was to test-fly repaired aircraft — to take an airframe that had been rebuilt and find out, before anyone else did, whether the work had held.

It meant flying whatever came through the shop. In practice that was most of the Navy’s jet inventory in the decade the fleet went from straight wings to swept, from guns to missiles, and from propellers to afterburners.

The list from the family record is the FH-1 Phantom, the F9F Panther, the F9F Cougar, the F2H Banshee, the F3H Demon and the A4D Skyhawk. Six aircraft, four manufacturers, and a span from the Navy’s first carrier jet to the attack aircraft that would still be flying forty years later.

He had come to it by way of an aeronautical engineering course at Georgia Tech, a war, and a squadron command. It is the assignment where the pilot and the engineer were doing the same job.

Aircraft on the test line
The Navy’s First Jet

McDonnell FH-1 Phantom

Twin-jet straight-wing fighter, and the first purely jet-powered aircraft to land aboard an American carrier. Only 62 were built; the design led directly to the Banshee and the Demon.

McDonnell was an unproven company when the Navy handed it the first American carrier jet contract in 1943, on the theory that the established builders were too busy. The result had two small Westinghouse J30 turbojets buried in the wing roots, leaving the fuselage clean and the nose free for guns. On 21 July 1946 an FH-1 flew off and landed back aboard USS Franklin D. Roosevelt, the first jet to do so from an American deck. It was quickly overtaken — only 62 were built and the type was gone by 1954 — but it started the line that produced the Banshee, the Demon, the Voodoo and the Phantom II.

Ted Landrum

Squadron and dates not yet recorded.

1950s · Carrier Jet Fighter

Grumman F9F Panther

Early straight-wing Navy carrier jet fighter.

Grumman’s first jet was conservative on purpose: a straight wing, permanent tip tanks, four 20mm cannon, and a license-built Rolls-Royce Nene for an engine. That conservatism made it dependable, and the Panther flew more combat sorties than any other Navy aircraft in Korea. On 9 November 1950 a Panther pilot shot down a MiG-15, the first air-to-air kill by a US Navy jet. The Blue Angels flew Panthers from 1949 to 1954.

Ted Landrum

Flown while assigned to the flight-test squadron at NAS Norfolk.

1950s · Carrier Jet Fighter

Grumman F9F Cougar (“F9 Cougar”)

Swept-wing development of the Panther, with improved high-speed performance.

Grumman took the Panther, swept the wing and tail back thirty-five degrees, and produced an aircraft that handled far better near the speed of sound — enough of a change that the Navy gave it a new name. The F9F-8 stretched the fuselage for more fuel and added a cambered leading edge that lowered the stall speed and improved handling at high angles of attack. It arrived too late for Korea. The two-seat F9F-8T trainer outlived them all, still flying with the Navy into the 1970s.

Ted Landrum

Flown while assigned to the flight-test squadron at NAS Norfolk.

1950s · Carrier Jet Fighter

McDonnell F2H Banshee

Navy carrier-based jet fighter that also flew fighter and ground-attack missions in Korea.

Descended directly from the FH-1 Phantom and powered by two Westinghouse J34s, the Banshee was bigger, longer-legged and much better at altitude than its contemporaries. In Korea it escorted bombers and flew the photo-reconnaissance work as the F2H-2P. It was also the only American jet fighter ever operated by the Royal Canadian Navy, which flew Banshees from HMCS Bonaventure until 1962.

Ted Landrum

Flown while assigned to the flight-test squadron at NAS Norfolk.

1950s · Carrier Jet Fighter

McDonnell F3H Demon

Subsonic Navy carrier jet fighter, forerunner in McDonnell’s fighter lineage to the later F-4 Phantom II.

The Demon was designed around the Westinghouse J40, an engine that failed so comprehensively that finished airframes sat waiting while the Navy re-engined the whole program with the Allison J71. What emerged was the Navy’s first fighter built around missiles rather than guns: a swept wing, all-weather radar, and Sparrows and Sidewinders on the rails. The lessons McDonnell took from it went straight into the Phantom II.

Ted Landrum

Flown while assigned to the flight-test squadron at NAS Norfolk.

Carrier Attack Jet

Douglas A-4 Skyhawk

Compact, single-seat carrier-based attack jet.

Ed Heinemann was told the Navy’s next attack aircraft could weigh 30,000 pounds. He delivered one at half that, with a delta wing short enough that it never needed a folding mechanism at all. Pilots called it the Scooter and the Tinker Toy Bomber; it was quick, agile and cheap. Production ran from 1954 to 1979, nearly three thousand aircraft, and Skyhawks were still flying in front-line service decades after the jets designed to replace them had retired.

Ted Landrum

Flown while assigned to the flight-test squadron at NAS Norfolk.

Atlanta to Dallas

The Civilian Years

Released to the inactive reserves, his first flying job was six months with National Airlines out of Miami. Then a regional grocery chain, Colonial Stores, hired him to fly a Twin Beech 18 taildragger out of Hartsfield. He stayed five years and never liked the drive in from Chamblee.

So when the Lay’s company went looking for a pilot for a Twin Beech they were about to buy, he applied. That put him at the Executive Aviation hangar at NAS Atlanta — Peachtree DeKalb — a few minutes from the house he and Florence owned in Chamblee. He flew their executives for about five years.

Lay’s moved up as he did: a Super 18 with tricycle gear, then a Rockwell 1121 Jet Commander, their first jet. In 1961 the merger with the Frito Company forced a move to Dallas, and he spent the next twenty-seven years flying the Frito-Lay fleet out of Love Field — a Canadair Challenger CL-604, an Astra SPX, and a Falcon.

Forty-odd years of flying, and the second half of it was spent moving executives on schedule and without incident. It is a less dramatic record than the first half, and a harder one to keep.

Aircraft of the civilian years
Civilian · Corporate Transport

Beechcraft Twin Beech 18

Twin-engine light transport; Landrum flew both the classic taildragger version and the tricycle-gear “Super 18.”

Beechcraft built the Model 18 from 1937 to 1969 — one of the longest production runs in aviation, more than nine thousand aircraft. Two Pratt & Whitney Wasp Junior radials, twin tails, room for six to eleven, and a reputation for hauling almost anything out of almost any field. It trained wartime navigators and bombardiers, flew freight and airline feeder routes, and for two decades was the standard corporate airplane in America. The Super 18 conversions swapped the tailwheel for tricycle gear.

Ted Landrum

Flown for Colonial Stores (Hartsfield-based) and then for Lay’s, based at Peachtree DeKalb / NAS Atlanta.

Business Jet

Rockwell (Jet Commander) 1121

Twin turbojet business jet, originally built by Aero Commander and later produced by Rockwell/IAI.

Ted Smith designed the 1121 with its two General Electric CJ610 turbojets hung at mid-fuselage and the wing passing straight through behind the cabin — unusual, but it left the cabin unbroken by a wing spar. Rockwell bought Aero Commander in 1967, then had to divest the program on antitrust grounds because it already owned Sabreliner. It went to Israel Aircraft Industries, which developed it into the Westwind and, eventually, the Astra.

Ted Landrum

Purchased by Lay’s after the Twin Beeches, flown out of Peachtree DeKalb.

Business Jet

Canadair / Bombardier Challenger (CL-604)

Mid/large-cabin twin-engine business jet.

The Challenger began as Bill Lear’s LearStar 600 and was bought by Canadair in 1976. Lear wanted a wide cabin a passenger could stand up in, matched to a supercritical wing that stayed efficient at high subsonic speed — a combination nobody in business aviation had attempted. The CL-604 was the mature version, with GE CF34 engines, a redesigned fuel system and transatlantic range. Stretch that same fuselage and you get the CRJ regional airliners.

Ted Landrum

Flown out of Love Field after the Lay’s/Frito-Lay merger forced his move to Dallas.

Business Jet

IAI Astra SPX (1125 Astra)

Israeli-built mid-size business jet.

The Astra is what the old Jet Commander became after thirty years of development in Israel. IAI kept the fuselage but moved the wing below the cabin floor and swept it, which freed the cabin entirely and improved cruise performance. The SPX added Honeywell TFE731-40R engines, winglets and a new interior. When Gulfstream acquired the line in 2001 the aircraft was renamed the G100.

Ted Landrum

Flown out of Love Field in Dallas during his 27 years flying for the Frito-Lay flight department.

Business Jet

Dassault Falcon (example: Falcon 20)

Twin-engine French business jet family. The biography does not specify the exact Falcon model he flew, so a representative Falcon 20 is shown.

Dassault’s first business jet flew in 1963, and its future was settled almost immediately when Pan American ordered forty with options on 120 more before the design was finished. Two rear-mounted General Electric CF700s, a cabin for eight to ten, and the handling of a company that mostly built fighters. Federal Express built its entire overnight network on Falcon 20 freighters in the 1970s. 512 were built before production ended in 1991.

Ted Landrum

Flown while living in Dallas, alongside the Challenger.

Afterward

Apalachicola

He bought the old Lay’s fishing house in Apalachicola, Florida, and for a few years taught student pilots with the Civil Air Patrol — back to primary instruction, where he had started at Pensacola in 1943.

He never gave up his naval commission. He held the rank of Commander, O-5, to the end.
